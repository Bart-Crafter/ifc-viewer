# Copyright, licences and liability when hosting project files

> **General information for Crafter Engineering, not legal advice.** It is written for an English-law company sharing project files. Before relying on it, have your solicitor and your professional-indemnity (PI) insurer confirm the points that matter to you.

## The situation
Our files often contain other people's material: an architect's or another consultant's drawing attached as an external reference (xref) or linked model, a client's drawing used as a base, Ordnance Survey (OS) or other mapping, a manufacturer's CAD block or logo, an extract from a British Standard, a photograph. Putting those files on this site copies them and makes them available to other people.

## What the law generally says
- **Drawings are protected automatically.** Under the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988, design drawings and plans are protected as literary/artistic works. The author owns the copyright, or their employer does if an employee made it in the course of employment. Nothing needs to be registered. See, for example, [Harper Macleod](https://www.harpermacleod.co.uk/insights/self-build-design-copyright/) and [Designing Buildings](https://www.designingbuildings.co.uk/wiki/Copyright%20of%20building%20design).
- **Copying and sharing without permission can infringe.** Reproducing another company's drawing, or making it available to others, without a licence is potentially infringement, even if it is only a reference inside our own drawing. A licence may be express (a contract or appointment) or implied from the circumstances, and an implied licence is usually narrow (for example "to use on this project") and can be challenged. Reference: [Harper Macleod](https://www.harpermacleod.co.uk/insights/self-build-design-copyright/).
- **Giving people access online can be "communicating to the public".** UK case law treats providing users with access to protected works as an act of communication, and platforms and their operators have been held liable alongside users (see [Bird & Bird's summary](https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2021/uk/copyright-communication-to-the-public-and-online-platform-liability)). Whether sharing with a closed project team is "to the public" depends on the facts; a **public QR code or a link sent to third parties looks more like public sharing** than a named client login.
- **The "hosting" defence probably doesn't help us.** The protection for online hosts generally applies to material supplied by their users, and only while they don't know about infringing content and remove it quickly when told. Here our own staff upload the files, so we are more likely to be treated as the one doing the sharing. (Check this point with a solicitor.)

## What it could mean for Crafter Engineering
- **A claim by the copyright owner:** an order to take the files down, damages or an account of profits, and costs. Not knowing doesn't stop liability for the act itself, though it can affect damages.
- **Breach of contract:** appointments, NDAs and BIM/information-exchange terms (for example ISO 19650 arrangements) often limit who may receive someone else's information and for what purpose. Sharing beyond that can be a breach even if copyright is not in dispute.
- **Third-party data licences:** OS licences generally don't allow raw licensed data to be passed to third parties who aren't themselves licensed. Passing finished drawings that incorporate OS data is usually acceptable, but a raw DWG with OS layers may not be. See [MapServe's overview](https://www.mapserve.co.uk/blog/os-licensing-explained-for-professionals) and OS's own [licensing pages](https://www.ordnancesurvey.co.uk/licensing). Check the licence you actually hold.
- **Confidentiality and personal data:** drawings can be confidential, and this site holds people's names and emails (UK GDPR).
- **Insurance:** many PI policies exclude or limit intellectual-property infringement. Ask your insurer.
- **Reputation and client trust,** especially if a consultant finds their drawing on a site anyone with a QR code can open.

## Where the risk is highest on this site
1. **Downloadable DWG / IFC** (editable, can carry xrefs, OS layers, other firms' layers) to people outside the licence chain.
2. **Public folders** reachable by anyone with the QR code (by default 01 – PDF and 03 – IFC). Anyone who can scan it sees those files.
3. **Quick-send links** to people we have no contract with.
4. **Unchecked uploads:** drawings supplied by others, with title blocks or logos left on.

## What the site now does to help
- **Rights confirmation:** before any upload or replacement the person must confirm Crafter has the right to share the file. The server refuses uploads without it, and the confirmation is recorded in the activity log with who and when.
- **Notices:** every project page, the sign-in page and the quick-link page carry a confidentiality/copyright notice and an invitation to report anything that shouldn't be there.
- **Reminders** when creating a quick link, and a clear explanation of what the QR code exposes.
- **Control and evidence:** per-folder access, an activity log, link reset, link cancellation, and one-click removal of files.

## What we recommend doing (policy, not software)
1. **Decide what may be shared and with whom.** Default to **PDF for anyone outside the project team**; keep DWG/IFC for people covered by the appointment or a licence.
2. **Before publishing a model/drawing,** bind or remove other companies' xrefs and layers (or publish to PDF), and remove OS or other licensed mapping unless the licence covers it.
3. **Check your appointments and the licences you hold** (client, architect, other consultants, OS/mapping, manufacturers' libraries). Add a licence-to-share clause to your standard appointment and subcontract terms if it isn't there.
4. **Keep sensitive or third-party material out of public folders;** switch folders off in *Who can see what* when in doubt, and reset the QR if it has spread.
5. **Have a takedown routine:** one named person receives complaints, removes the file the same day, and records it. Note that deleted files stay in the SharePoint recycle bin and version history until purged; if a file must really disappear, delete it from the recycle bin and the library's versions too.
6. **Tell your PI insurer** how project files are shared and ask whether IP claims are covered.
7. **Get a solicitor to review** the wording of the notice and your terms once, so you can rely on it.
